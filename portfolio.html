Section 01

Introduction

The Innovation

This portfolio examines IoT-Based Smart Traffic Signal Preemption — an intelligent transportation system designed to clear green corridors for emergency vehicles in real time. The system integrates GPS-enabled emergency vehicles, edge-computing nodes at intersections, cloud-based AI routing, and programmable IoT traffic signals into a unified, low-latency response network.

Organization & Context

The innovation targets the City of Orlando, Florida — one of the fastest-growing metro areas in the United States. With a 30% population surge since 2015, average emergency response times have climbed to 9 minutes, well above the critical 4-minute cardiac survival threshold. Orlando's 600+ signalized intersections represent both the bottleneck and the opportunity: modernizing them with IoT preemption technology could compress response windows from 9 minutes to 7 minutes or fewer, directly translating to improved survival outcomes for cardiac, trauma, and fire emergencies.

Real-World Relevance

Signal preemption is not a theoretical concept — systems like Opticom by GTT Solutions have been deployed in cities across the US and Canada, using infrared or GPS-based vehicle detection. What distinguishes the Orlando proposal is the edge-computing + AI routing hybrid: rather than simple detection-and-hold logic, it uses predictive corridor planning, allowing the system to pre-stage signals several intersections ahead of the vehicle's current position — a meaningful architectural leap over legacy preemption.

Tier 1 — Vehicle GPS Unit

Sub-meter GPS hardware (typically u-blox M9 or equivalent) transmits position, heading, speed, and vehicle ID at 1–4 Hz intervals. Communication uses dedicated short-range communication (DSRC, 5.9 GHz 802.11p) or LTE-V2X (C-V2X, 3GPP Release 14+) as a fallback. Packet payloads are small (≈200 bytes), prioritized with DSCP QoS marking for low latency.

DSRC / C-V2X 802.11p GPS NMEA

Tier 2 — Edge Computing Node

A ruggedized compute unit at each intersection (e.g., Intel NUC-class or NVIDIA Jetson Nano) runs a local preemption decision engine. When a vehicle is detected within a configurable radius (typically 500–1500 m), the node computes time-to-arrival, evaluates current phase state, and issues a preemption request to the signal controller. End-to-end edge latency target: <50 ms. Nodes maintain a local state machine and can operate autonomously if cloud connectivity is lost.

NEMA TS2 Controller NTCIP 1202 Edge ML

Tier 3 — Cloud AI Routing Engine

A cloud-hosted service (Kubernetes-orchestrated microservices) ingests real-time GPS streams, live traffic density data, and historical pattern data to compute optimal corridor routing. The AI component — likely a graph neural network or modified Dijkstra variant with dynamic edge weights — pre-stages preemption requests along the predicted route. It publishes planned phase-change sequences via MQTT to a broker, which edge nodes subscribe to.

MQTT Kubernetes Graph NN

Tier 4 — IoT Signal Controller

Modern signal controllers (Model 2070 or ASC/3) support NTCIP (National Transportation Communications for ITS Protocol) over TCP/IP, enabling remote phase control. A preemption request overrides the normal cycle plan: the controller holds the approaching direction on green (or transitions it from red) while cross-street phases receive extended red holds. After the vehicle clears, the controller executes a recovery sequence to restore normal timing.

Security Architecture — The Gap Our Team Missed

Our group presentation mentioned "end-to-end encryption" as a single mitigation bullet, but the threat model for a system that physically controls traffic signals is substantially more complex and deserves serious analysis.

Key Attack Vectors
GPS Spoofing: An attacker broadcasting a fake GPS signal could trick a legitimate vehicle unit into transmitting false position data, triggering unwarranted preemption across a corridor — effectively creating a denial-of-service against normal traffic flow. MQTT Broker Compromise: If the cloud broker is compromised, an attacker could publish malicious phase-change sequences to all subscribed edge nodes simultaneously. Replay Attacks: Capturing and replaying valid preemption packets could trigger false activations.

Mitigations beyond what our team described include: cryptographic vehicle identity (PKI-based certificates per vehicle, rotated regularly), plausibility filtering at edge nodes (reject position jumps inconsistent with physics), mutual TLS for MQTT, anomaly detection on preemption frequency per vehicle ID, and air-gapped backup control at each intersection.

Scalability & Performance Considerations

At full city-wide deployment (600 intersections), the cloud routing engine must handle concurrent emergencies from multiple vehicles across overlapping corridors. This creates a multi-vehicle conflict resolution problem — if two ambulances request preemption on intersecting routes, the system needs a priority queue and conflict graph resolution logic. Our team did not address this scenario at all, and it represents a significant engineering challenge, particularly during mass casualty events when many units are dispatched simultaneously.

Edge nodes mitigate cloud latency by acting as autonomous fallbacks, but their local decision-making is limited to their own intersection's state — they cannot coordinate across the corridor without cloud connectivity, meaning network outages degrade the system from "predictive corridor" mode to legacy "reactive hold" mode.

Section 04

Critical Evaluation

The following analyzes the innovation from four distinct perspectives, including limitations and risks that our group presentation did not adequately address.

Technical Perspective

  • GPS accuracy degrades in urban canyons (Orlando's downtown high-rises may interfere with sub-meter precision at critical intersections)
  • The 50 ms edge latency target is aggressive; real-world cellular backhaul may introduce 80–150 ms, causing phase-change timing errors
  • No mention of software update / firmware management strategy for 600 distributed edge nodes — a significant operational security surface
  • Multi-vehicle corridor conflicts are unaddressed — a critical gap for mass-casualty events
  • 15–20% response time reduction claim lacks a cited peer-reviewed source specific to Orlando's road topology

Organizational Perspective

  • Integration with Orlando's existing SCOOT/SCATS traffic management software requires significant API negotiation — legacy systems may not support NTCIP 1202 without hardware replacement
  • Requires sustained buy-in from three distinct entities: Orlando DOT, Orange County Fire Rescue, and FDOT — each with separate procurement cycles and political priorities
  • Operator training must cover both the automated system and manual override protocols — our team underestimated the depth of training change management required
  • Vendor lock-in risk is high: if the city commits to one signal controller vendor's IoT stack, future upgrades become expensive

Ethical Perspective

  • Phased deployment in "high-priority corridors" likely means tourist/commercial corridors first — communities in East and West Orlando may wait years for equivalent coverage, raising equity concerns
  • Persistent GPS tracking of emergency vehicles creates movement data; even anonymized fleet data could reveal station locations, patrol patterns, or operational vulnerabilities if breached
  • False activations (system triggered by a non-emergency GPS spoof or software bug) disrupt civilian traffic and could create secondary accidents
  • Algorithmic routing decisions are opaque — communities may not understand why their intersection is held red while others are prioritized

Societal Perspective

  • Aggregate improvement is real but unevenly distributed — 15–20% reduction city-wide may mask neighborhoods that see little improvement during the pilot phase
  • System dependency risk: over time, dispatchers may reduce driver discretion in favor of automation, degrading human response capacity if the system fails
  • Public trust implications of city infrastructure controlled via networked software — a high-profile cyberattack or malfunction would damage confidence in smart city initiatives broadly
  • Positive: the data exhaust of this system (intersection-level delay, vehicle throughput) is a genuine public good for long-term infrastructure planning
Section 05

Integration of Computer Science Knowledge

This system draws on at least five distinct areas of computer science, each playing a critical functional role in the architecture.

Networking & Protocols

The system's real-time data pipeline depends on carefully chosen networking protocols. DSRC (802.11p) provides ultra-low latency V2X communication at the physical intersection layer, with round-trip times under 10 ms — critical for preemption timing. LTE-C-V2X (3GPP Rel.14) acts as a cellular fallback. MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport), a publish-subscribe protocol designed for constrained IoT devices, carries phase-change commands from the cloud broker to edge subscribers. Quality of Service (QoS Level 2 — exactly once delivery) is essential here: a duplicate phase-change command at the wrong moment could hold an intersection on green indefinitely. TCP/IP with NTCIP sits at the intersection controller layer for standards-compliant signal management.

Distributed Systems

This is fundamentally a distributed real-time system — geographically dispersed nodes must make low-latency decisions with partial information and tolerate network partitions gracefully. The edge nodes implement a variant of the CAP theorem trade-off: during cloud connectivity loss, they sacrifice Consistency (no corridor-level coordination) to maintain Availability (local preemption still works). The cloud routing engine must handle concurrent state from hundreds of simultaneous sensors. Concepts like event-driven architecture, message brokers (Kafka or MQTT), and distributed consensus protocols for conflict resolution are all directly applicable. The multi-vehicle conflict problem maps neatly to distributed priority queue management.

Cybersecurity & Cryptography

Because the system controls physical infrastructure, security is not optional. PKI (Public Key Infrastructure) must issue and rotate X.509 certificates for each vehicle unit, enabling mutual authentication with edge nodes. TLS 1.3 encrypts all MQTT payloads. GPS anti-spoofing requires signal authentication (OSNMA for Galileo, or proprietary u-blox AssureIT). At the application layer, HMAC message signing on preemption packets prevents replay attacks. Operating system hardening on edge nodes (minimized attack surface, read-only root filesystems, SELinux MAC policies) reduces the blast radius of a compromised node. This maps directly to coursework in cryptography, secure protocols, and operating systems security.

Artificial Intelligence & Algorithms

The AI routing component is the system's most computationally interesting element. At its core, it is a dynamic shortest-path problem on a weighted graph where edge weights (intersection delay times) update continuously. A modified Dijkstra or A* algorithm with live weight feeds handles baseline routing. The predictive layer — pre-staging phase changes ahead of the vehicle — requires forecasting: how long will the current phase hold? What is the vehicle's ETA at the next intersection given current speed and signal state? Regression models (or LSTM networks trained on historical phase duration data) can answer this. In multi-vehicle scenarios, the conflict resolution engine is essentially a constraint satisfaction problem, solvable with local search or branch-and-bound approaches covered in AI coursework.

Databases & Data Engineering

The analytics platform requires a multi-model database strategy. Real-time telemetry (GPS positions, phase states) suits a time-series database like InfluxDB or TimescaleDB, optimized for high-frequency write throughput and time-windowed aggregations. Historical incident records (response times per incident, per corridor, per time of day) belong in a relational store (PostgreSQL) for complex ad-hoc analytics. A geospatial layer (PostGIS) indexes intersection polygons and vehicle route paths for geographic queries. Data retention policies and anonymization pipelines are needed to prevent vehicle tracking data from becoming a privacy liability after its operational use ends. This maps to database design, data modeling, and data engineering principles.

Operating Systems & Embedded Systems

Edge computing nodes run on constrained hardware with strict real-time requirements. A Real-Time Operating System (RTOS) — or a Linux kernel with PREEMPT_RT patches — is needed to guarantee deterministic scheduling for the preemption decision loop. The vehicle GPS unit firmware must handle sensor fusion (GPS + IMU dead-reckoning for urban canyon gaps) and transmit data with bounded jitter. Memory management is critical: heap fragmentation over months of uptime on an embedded device can introduce unpredictable latency spikes in the preemption logic — a safety-critical failure mode. OS-level concepts of interrupt handling, scheduling priorities, and memory isolation apply directly.

Section 06

Reflection

Growth Arc
Group work → Individual understanding → Future vision. This section traces that arc honestly.
What did you learn individually from this innovation?

I learned that what looks like a simple "GPS + signal" system is actually a layered distributed real-time architecture with non-trivial security, concurrency, and protocol-design challenges. Working through the technical deep dive independently forced me to understand the why behind architectural choices — why edge nodes exist (latency), why MQTT over HTTP (protocol overhead at IoT scale), why DSRC over standard WiFi (dedicated spectrum, zero interference). I also learned to think in threat models: every system that controls physical infrastructure has to be designed as if an adversary is actively trying to abuse it.

How did your understanding change after the group presentation?

During the group project, I saw myself as the "big picture person" — the problem framer. After revisiting the system individually, I realize that framing the problem well is only valuable if the technical solution you propose can actually deliver. Our presentation projected confident statistics (15–20% response time reduction) that, on deeper examination, are drawn from general ITS literature and may not map cleanly onto Orlando's specific road topology, intersection density, and legacy controller hardware. My understanding shifted from advocacy to critical engineering judgment.

What new perspective or vision do you now have?

I now see this innovation not just as a public safety tool, but as the first node in a broader urban intelligence network. The same IoT and edge-computing infrastructure that enables emergency preemption could — with appropriate data governance — support adaptive signal timing for all vehicles, pedestrian crossing optimization via computer vision, real-time air quality sensing, and predictive road maintenance alerts. The $30M investment in signal infrastructure is not a one-purpose spend; it is platform infrastructure for the next two decades of Orlando's smart city evolution.

What would you do differently if redoing the project?

I would insist on a more grounded technical specification early in the process. The group converged quickly on high-level architecture and moved to cost modeling, but we never answered: which specific signal controller protocol? What is the decision latency of the edge node under load? How does the system behave when two vehicles conflict? Without those answers, the $25K-per-intersection cost estimate is not well-founded. I would also have proposed a simulation model — even a simple Python graph simulation — to test the corridor pre-staging algorithm's response time claims before presenting them as projected improvements.

What did the team miss or underexplore?

Three significant gaps: (1) Cybersecurity depth — our single bullet point on encryption vastly underrepresents a safety-critical threat surface. (2) Multi-vehicle conflict resolution — the system as described cannot handle simultaneous emergencies on intersecting corridors without additional logic we never specified. (3) Equity analysis — we claimed the system "benefits all neighborhoods" without examining whether phased rollout creates a period of inequitable coverage, or whether certain communities face structural barriers to benefiting from improved response times due to other infrastructure gaps.

What opportunities exist beyond what the team identified?

The most compelling unexplored opportunity is V2X pedestrian integration: the same cellular-V2X infrastructure used for vehicle preemption can broadcast "pedestrian in crosswalk" warnings from smartphone apps or wearables, allowing edge nodes to extend pedestrian crossing time automatically. For a city with Florida's pedestrian fatality rates (consistently among the highest in the nation), this is a high-impact addition. A second opportunity: using the system's historical data to build ML models that predict when and where emergencies are most likely to occur by time of day, enabling pre-positioning of units — shifting from reactive to proactive emergency management.

How close is this innovation to real-world implementation?

Closer than most smart city proposals. The core components — GPS fleet tracking, NTCIP-compliant signal controllers, LTE connectivity, cloud analytics — are all commercially available today. Opticom by GTT has deployments in hundreds of US cities. What is less mature is the AI corridor pre-staging at scale and the edge-cloud integration for multi-vehicle conflict resolution. I would estimate the individual components at TRL 7–8 (system prototype demonstrated in operational environment), while the integrated Orlando-specific system as proposed is TRL 4–5 (technology validated in lab, not yet in operational environment). A realistic pilot timeline is 18–24 months from funding approval to first deployment on 20–30 intersections.

What are the main barriers?

Technical: Legacy signal controller compatibility — many of Orlando's older intersections may require full hardware replacement, not just software updates, pushing the real per-intersection cost above the $25K estimate. Organizational: Multi-agency coordination across Orlando DOT, Orange County, FDOT, and multiple EMS agencies with different procurement timelines and political cycles. Ethical: Community trust — particularly in neighborhoods with historical tensions with law enforcement, real-time vehicle tracking by city infrastructure may face public resistance. Societal: Sustaining political will and budget through the 3–5 year ROI window, during which the city bears full costs without yet seeing full benefits.

What is one meaningful next step or future direction?

The single most impactful next step would be a digital twin simulation of Orlando's signalized corridor network. Using open data from Orange County's GIS system and FDOT traffic counts, a team could build a graph model of the city's intersection network and simulate the preemption algorithm under realistic traffic conditions — validating (or disproving) the 15–20% response time reduction claim before any hardware is purchased. This would cost tens of thousands of dollars rather than tens of millions, provide defensible evidence for a grant application, and surface the multi-vehicle conflict scenarios that current planning has not addressed. It is the kind of evidence-first approach that would make this proposal genuinely compelling to city decision-makers rather than aspirationally compelling.
